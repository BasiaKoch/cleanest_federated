\begin{abstract}
Federated learning lets institutions train a shared model without pooling private
data, but real federations are heterogeneous in both their data and their compute,
and medical image datasets are severely class-imbalanced. This thesis studies
federated optimisation on DermaMNIST, a seven-class dermatology benchmark
dominated by a single benign class, comparing FedAvg, FedProx, and FedNova across
controlled statistical- and system-heterogeneity regimes and reporting test
macro-F1 at the best-validation checkpoint. Rather than crowning a universal
winner, it maps the regimes in which each method preserves or loses rare-class
signal. The central finding is that under Li-style straggler asymmetry the FedProx
advantage (about $+0.115$ macro-F1) is carried almost entirely by its
$\gamma$-inexact acceptance of partial updates rather than by the proximal term,
and that FedNova is robust under deterministic learning-rate asymmetry yet
collapses under random per-round stragglers. Across the failing regimes, rare
lesions are misrouted into the majority class. These results come from a single
low-resolution benchmark with simulated heterogeneity and $n=3$ mechanism probes,
and establish no universal optimiser ranking.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Diagnostic models improve with large, diverse image collections, but in medicine
those collections are fragmented across institutions and cannot usually be pooled
centrally because of privacy regulation and governance. Federated learning
sidesteps this by keeping each institution's data in place and exchanging only
model updates \citep{mcmahan2017fedavg, rieke2020future, sheller2020medicalfl},
which makes it a natural setting for collaborative learning across hospitals
\citep{kairouz2021advances}. It also introduces two difficulties that are acute
in medicine. Under \emph{statistical heterogeneity}, sites see different case
mixes, so client data is non-IID; under \emph{system heterogeneity}, sites differ
in compute and availability, so some clients return only partial updates in a
round. The two coincide most damagingly when the under-resourced site is also the
one holding the rare cases.

Medical data is, in addition, severely class-imbalanced. DermaMNIST
\citep{yang2023medmnistv2}, derived from the HAM10000 dermatoscopic collection
\citep{tschandl2018ham10000} and used throughout this thesis, is dominated by
benign melanocytic nevi, while the diagnostically urgent lesions are rare. A model
can therefore post high accuracy while failing on exactly the lesions that matter,
so we report macro-F1 and per-class rare-class diagnostics rather than accuracy.
Crucially, the information needed to recognise a rare lesion reaches the global
model only through the updates of the client that holds it; if heterogeneity
under-weights or discards those updates, the rare-class signal is lost. The
quantity that governs rare-class performance is thus the \emph{flow of
rare-client signal into the global model}, and the federated optimiser --- not the
network architecture --- is the lever that protects or forfeits it.

\section{Research problem}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

The optimisers proposed for these conditions --- FedAvg \citep{mcmahan2017fedavg},
FedProx with its proximal term and tolerance for inexact updates
\citep{li2020fedprox}, and FedNova with its work-normalised aggregation
\citep{wang2020fednova} --- are usually compared by which scores higher on a task
\citep{li2022niidbench}, rarely isolating \emph{why}, often conflating the two
heterogeneities, and judging by accuracy. This thesis instead asks \emph{when}
each method preserves rare-class performance, treating the answer as a
\emph{regime map} rather than a universal ranking. It separates four levers that
are usually bundled together --- the data partition, the optimiser, the
partial-update protocol, and loss-side imbalance correction --- and varies them one
at a time. All experiments are simulated federated learning on a controlled
benchmark, so the work maps mechanisms rather than validating a clinical
deployment.

\section{Research questions and contributions}
\label{sec:intro-questions}

This framing raises four questions, examined in turn in
Chapter \ref{ch:results}. Because the literature often credits FedProx and
FedNova with broad gains, the thesis first asks whether statistical heterogeneity
alone is enough to separate FedAvg and FedProx or whether additional stress is
required; then, where FedProx does help under stragglers, whether that benefit
comes from its proximal regularisation or from its $\gamma$-inexact acceptance of
partial updates; whether FedNova's work-normalisation is robust in general or
only under deterministic learning-rate asymmetry, failing under random
round-to-round straggling; and finally whether rare-class collapse into the
majority class is the shared failure mode, and whether the FedProx advantage is
merely class-imbalance correction in disguise.

Answering these yields the contributions of the thesis. It maps the regimes in
which FedAvg, FedProx, and FedNova preserve or lose rare-class signal; it
decomposes the FedProx straggler-regime advantage into its proximal and
$\gamma$-inexact components using matched class-disjoint (L4) and quantity-only
(L1) partitions that fix when the effect can appear; it characterises FedNova's
regime-dependence across deterministic and random system heterogeneity; it
identifies rare-class collapse into the dominant melanocytic-nevi class as the
common failure mode; and it shows, through a loss-side comparison, that the
FedProx advantage is a drift-and-protocol effect rather than class-imbalance
correction in disguise.

\section{Problem formulation and notation}
\label{sec:method-problem}

We consider a federation of $K$ clients coordinated by a central server that
together train a single global model without exchanging raw data
(Figure~\ref{fig:fl-workflow}). Client $i$
holds a private training set $D_i$ of $n_i$ examples, with $N = \sum_i n_i$. In
each round $t$ the server broadcasts the global model $w^{t}$; every
participating client runs $E$ epochs of local SGD on $D_i$ to obtain $w_i^{t+1}$
and returns its update $d_i = w_i^{t+1} - w^{t}$; the baseline rule, FedAvg
\citep{mcmahan2017fedavg}, recombines these by the sample-weighted mean
$w^{t+1} = \sum_i (n_i / N)\, w_i^{t+1}$. FedProx \citep{li2020fedprox} and FedNova
\citep{wang2020fednova} modify, respectively, the local objective and the way the
updates $d_i$ are normalised before combination
(\S\ref{sec:method-optimisers}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{F_federated_learning_workflow.pdf}
\caption{The federated learning loop (conceptual). Each round, the server
broadcasts the global model; clients train locally on their own private data and
return model updates --- weights, not raw data --- which the server aggregates
into the next global model, repeating for $R$ rounds. This thesis
\emph{simulates} this process on a single machine rather than deploying a real
multi-hospital federation; keeping data local is a structural property of the
protocol shown here, not a formal privacy guarantee.}
\label{fig:fl-workflow}
\end{figure}

The federation departs from the IID case along two independent axes.
\emph{Statistical heterogeneity} concerns which classes each client owns and in
what proportion --- from IID, through Dirichlet label skew
\citep{hsu2019dirichlet}, to class-disjoint clients --- and is fixed before
training by the data partition; it determines \emph{where} rare-class information
resides. \emph{System heterogeneity} concerns how much, and how reliably, each
client contributes: a straggler completes fewer local steps, its partial update
may be dropped or accepted, or it may take smaller steps under a lower learning
rate. It determines \emph{whether} a client's information reaches aggregation. The
two interact most sharply when the client carrying the rare classes is also the
one that is slow or under-weighted.

This motivates the concept that organises the thesis, \emph{rare-client signal
flow}: the per-round contribution that the client holding rare-class examples
makes to the global update. A rare class is learned only to the extent that the
information in that client's update $d_i$ reaches and persists in $w^{t+1}$, so
the organising hypothesis is that rare-class performance is preserved precisely
when rare-client signal continues to reach the global model. Two matched
two-client partitions isolate this, shown schematically in
Figure~\ref{fig:l1-l4-schematic}: the class-disjoint \textbf{L4}, in which one
client uniquely holds melanoma, dermatofibroma, and vascular lesions, and its
quantity-only control \textbf{L1}, in which both clients carry near-global
proportions --- so suppressing a client removes unique rare-class signal under L4
but not under L1. Performance is measured throughout by test macro-F1 at the
best-validation checkpoint and by the rare-class F1 over
$\mathrm{RARE\_IDX} = \{\text{dermatofibroma}, \text{melanoma}, \text{vascular}\}$,
because accuracy is dominated by the majority class and stays high even when the
rare-class signal has been lost. The remainder of this chapter specifies how this
setting is instantiated.

\section{Experimental design rationale}
\label{sec:method-rationale}

This thesis studies federated learning on a class-imbalanced medical
image-classification benchmark under both statistical and system
heterogeneity. The aim is not to crown a universal winner among FedAvg
\citep{mcmahan2017fedavg}, FedProx \citep{li2020fedprox}, and FedNova
\citep{wang2020fednova}, but to map which regimes preserve or lose
rare-class performance.

The design separates three independent axes that the FL literature
often conflates. \emph{Statistical heterogeneity} controls which clients
own the rare-class signal --- IID, engineered, Dirichlet, and
class-disjoint partitions concentrate rare-class examples to differing
degrees. \emph{System heterogeneity} controls whether that signal
reaches aggregation: drop-stragglers handling discards updates from
clients with fewer local steps, while $\gamma$-inexact handling accepts
those partial updates. \emph{Optimiser and protocol choices} then
determine the global response: vanilla averaging (FedAvg),
proximal-anchored local training (FedProx), and effective-step
normalisation (FedNova). The chapter follows a causal-contrast
sequence that the Results chapter walks paragraph for paragraph:
IID baseline $\to$ engineered statistical-heterogeneity partitions
$\to$ $\mu$-sweep $\to$ L1/L4 four-condition decomposition $\to$
learning-rate-asymmetry envelope $\to$ loss-side comparators.

Several claims are deliberately \emph{not} made. The simulator does
not emulate a real hospital network: no wall-clock deadlines, no
privacy or communication-cost budgets, no clinical validation. The
optimiser set is not an exhaustive FL benchmark (no SCAFFOLD, FedDyn,
or FedAdam). The four-condition decomposition lacks a FedAvg +
$\gamma$-inexact cell, so the attribution reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is for the \emph{FedProx + $\gamma$-inexact
protocol}, not a proof that $\gamma$-inexact acceptance is universally
beneficial as a standalone mechanism.

\section{Dataset and rare-class failure motivation}
\label{sec:method-dataset}

All experiments use DermaMNIST from MedMNIST~v2
\citep{yang2023medmnistv2}, a seven-class dermatology benchmark derived
from the HAM10000 dataset of dermatoscopic skin-lesion images
\citep{tschandl2018ham10000}. The task is single-image classification at
$28 \times 28$ RGB resolution. Splits follow the canonical MedMNIST~v2
release with $7007$ training, $1003$ validation, and $2005$ test images.
Images are loaded from the packaged \texttt{dermamnist\_64.npz} array,
permuted to channel-first float, scaled to $[0, 1]$, bilinearly resized to
$28 \times 28$, and normalised with ImageNet channel statistics.

The benchmark is severely class-imbalanced (Table~\ref{tab:dataset-classes}):
the dominant melanocytic-nevi class (mel-nevi) accounts for
$66.9\,\%$ of the training set and $1341 / 2005$ test images. The two
smallest classes --- dermatofibroma ($1.1\,\%$, $23$ test images) and
vascular lesions ($1.4\,\%$, $29$ test) --- have so few support that any
optimiser that fails to learn them suffers a large macro-F1 penalty
while keeping raw accuracy almost unchanged. Melanoma ($11.1\,\%$,
$223$ test) is mid-support but is included in the canonical rare-class
group used throughout this thesis,
$\mathrm{RARE\_IDX} = \{\text{dermato}, \text{melanoma}, \text{vascular}\}$,
because missing a melanoma in a clinical screening setting is the highest
priority failure the dataset can express. Rare-class F1 is reported as
the unweighted mean of per-class F1 over these three classes; the
$0.265$ value used in earlier drafts that excluded melanoma is not used.

The remaining four classes --- actinic keratoses, basal cell carcinoma,
benign keratosis-like lesions, and the dominant mel-nevi --- are reported
per-class but do not have a special role: they are neither the smallest
nor the highest-priority. Mel-nevi acts throughout the chapter as the
\emph{majority attractor}: under several failing optimiser/protocol
combinations, rare-class images are disproportionately routed into
mel-nevi or other majority-class predictions, allowing raw accuracy
to remain misleadingly high while macro-F1 collapses.

Client partitions, defined in Section~\ref{sec:method-partitions}, apply
to the training set only. The validation and test sets remain global and
class-stratified across every experiment; validation drives the best-val
checkpoint selector and test is held out for the final reported macro-F1.
This separation means that any reported test-set rare-class collapse is
not a partition artefact --- the test set always contains all seven
classes in the supports tabulated below.

\section{Federated simulation setup}
\label{sec:method-simulation}

All experiments are simulated federated learning, not a real-hospital
deployment. Each run executes the loop of Figure~\ref{fig:fl-workflow} for
$R = 150$ rounds with $E = 20$ local epochs of mini-batch SGD per client, using
the same global, class-stratified validation and test sets throughout --- only
the client training partition varies.

Two federation sizes are used:
\begin{description}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item[7-client federation] carries the headline 10-seed benchmarks
    (IID, engineered balanced-paired, Dirichlet $\alpha = 0.1$,
    specialist 1-of-7, Flower $C = 1$ baseline) and the random-straggler
    system-het comparisons including the FedNova random-$\tau$ collapse
    experiment.
  \item[2-client federation] carries the controlled mechanism probes
    that need a clean rare-client vs dominant-client interaction: L1
    and L4 four-condition decompositions, perfect-storm, LR-asymmetry
    envelope, weighted-CE / focal comparators, asymmetric $\mu$,
    node-pinned L4, ladder pilot.
\end{description}
No claim of cross-scale generalisation between the two federation sizes
is made.

Two training regimes are used:
\begin{description}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item[Regime A] (7-client default): SGD with momentum $0.9$, batch
    $32$, $\textrm{lr} = 0.01$, weight decay $0$, $E = 20$, $R = 150$.
  \item[Regime B] (Li-style plain-SGD, 2-client mechanism probes):
    momentum $0$, batch $10$, same lr / wd / $E$ / $R$.
\end{description}
The four-condition decomposition additionally fixes Client 1's
$E_{\textrm{straggler}} = 5$. Each experiment's regime is stated in
the corresponding Results section. The best-validation-checkpoint
selector records the round of peak validation macro-F1; test macro-F1
in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is always evaluated at that checkpoint.

\section{Model architecture and local training}
\label{sec:method-model}

The model is a small CNN used as the benchmark vehicle and is not a
contribution of this thesis. Four convolutional blocks of widths
$32 \to 64 \to 128 \to 256$ each pair a $3 \times 3$ convolution with
GroupNorm \citep{wu2018groupnorm} and ReLU; max-pooling separates the
first three blocks and adaptive average pooling follows the fourth, then
a $256 \to 128$ dense layer with dropout $0.2$ feeds the seven-class
head. GroupNorm is used rather than BatchNorm because BN's per-batch
statistics diverge across heterogeneous federated clients and its
running buffers leak distribution information between rounds, a
well-documented non-IID FL pathology \citep{hsieh2020quagmire}. The local objective is
unweighted cross-entropy unless §\ref{sec:method-losses} overrides;
local SGD uses the active regime defined in
§\ref{sec:method-simulation}, with best-validation checkpointing.

\section{Client partition designs}
\label{sec:method-partitions}

Partition design is the chapter's primary lever for statistical
heterogeneity: it determines whether each client sees a near-global
class mix, or rare-class signal is concentrated on a few clients --- or,
in the strongest mechanism probes, on a single client whose
contribution can then be made to fail or succeed by varying the
system-heterogeneity protocol. Seven partitions are used; their roles
and downstream consumers are in Table~\ref{tab:partition-designs}.

\subsection{IID control}

The IID partition is a negative control: every client receives an
independent random sample of the training set with the global class
proportions. A substantial FedProx advantage over FedAvg here would
not support the heterogeneity-correction interpretation
\citep{li2020fedprox} and would require implementation scrutiny. This
is the falsification check for §\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity}.

\subsection{Engineered balanced-paired 7-client partition}

The engineered balanced-paired partition is a controlled
statistical-heterogeneity benchmark that sits between the IID baseline
above and the singleton specialist case below: every minority class is
concentrated on exactly two clients (so rare-class signal is unevenly
shared) but no minority class is isolated to a single client (so the
proximal term still has a paired client to align with). It is the
substrate for the headline FedAvg/FedProx comparison and the
$\mu$-sweep, and is the only $7$-client partition in which rare-class
ownership is deterministic rather than stochastic (cf.\ Dirichlet) or
singleton (cf.\ specialist 1-of-7).

The construction is fixed and deterministic, and is
visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:engineered-partition}: minority classes
are paired across clients (actinic / basal on C0--C1, benign-keratosis
/ dermatofibroma on C2--C3, melanoma / vascular on C4--C5), mel-nevi
is distributed in $\approx 670$-image shares across all seven clients,
and C6 is a mel-nevi-only generalist. The resulting per-client
training sizes are $964 / 963 / 1095 / 1094 / 1110 / 1108 / 673$.
This partition is \emph{not} the two-client $90/10$ partition; the
$90/10$ label is reserved for the L4 mechanism partition below.

\centering

The partition is deliberately structured. By the author's own design
note, it was \emph{``designed to maximise FedProx's advantage on the
per-class drift mechanism''}; the small Flower-paired $\Delta$
reported in §\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity} should therefore be
read against this FedProx-favouring framing rather than as a
naturalistic hospital deployment. The point is not to mimic any
particular hospital network but to provide a controlled middle-ground
setting in which to test whether FedProx helps when rare-class
information is unevenly --- but not singleton --- shared across
clients.

\subsection{Two-client L1/L4 mechanism partitions}

L1 and L4 are matched two-client controls. Both use the same federation size,
the same approximate (86/14) sample split, and the same protocol family.
They differ in one variable: whether Client~1 carries class information that
Client~0 lacks.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{F_l1_l4_partition_schematic.pdf}
\caption{Two-client mechanism partitions. \textbf{L1} keeps the (86/14)
quantity split but gives both clients near-global class proportions.
\textbf{L4} is class-disjoint: Client~0 holds the common classes and
Client~1 uniquely holds melanoma, dermatofibroma, and vascular lesions.
The contrast isolates whether the under-trained client carries unique

\label{fig:l1-l4-schematic}
\end{figure}

\emph{L4} is class-disjoint. Client~0 holds mel-nevi,
benign-keratosis-like lesions, actinic keratoses, and basal cell carcinoma.
Client~1 holds melanoma, dermatofibroma, and vascular lesions. The realised
split is $86/14$, not literally $90/10$; the name
\texttt{two\_client\_90\_10\_rare\_stress} is a filename legacy. JS divergence
between clients is $1.0$. L4 is used for the four-condition decomposition,
perfect-storm, asymmetric-LR, and loss-side comparator experiments.

\emph{L1} is the matched-quantity control. It keeps the same $86/14$
quantity skew, but every class is stratified across both clients in
near-global proportions; JS divergence is approximately $5 \times 10^{-6}$.
L1 therefore tests whether $\gamma$-inexact partial-update acceptance helps
generically, while L4 tests whether it helps specifically when the
under-trained client holds unique rare-class signal.

Partitions define where rare-class information resides. The next two
sections define how optimiser and protocol choices determine whether that
information reaches aggregation.

\section{Federated optimisation methods}
\label{sec:method-optimisers}

The chapter treats optimiser choice and straggler-handling protocol as
\emph{independent} design axes. FedAvg, FedProx, and FedNova define how
each client's local update is produced and combined; drop versus
$\gamma$-inexact handling (§\ref{sec:method-protocols}) separately
determines whether a straggler's partial update enters aggregation. The
same optimiser can be paired with either protocol, and vice versa ---
the separation is what allows the four-condition decomposition in
§\ref{sec:li-straggler} to attribute the rare-class-rescue effect to
one mechanism rather than the other. Table~\ref{tab:optimisers}
summarises the three optimisers, their mechanism, main knob, expected
vulnerability, and the Results section that probes it.

\subsection{FedAvg}

FedAvg \citep{mcmahan2017fedavg} is the baseline against which the other
two methods are read. Clients run $E$ epochs of local SGD, and the server
aggregates by sample-weighted mean $w_{t+1} = \sum_i (n_i / N) w_i^{t+1}$:
no drift control, no effective-work normalisation. A direct consequence
on L4 is that the rare-class client ($\approx 14\,\%$ of training
samples) is structurally under-weighted: the dominant client supplies
$\approx 86\,\%$ of the gradient mass.

\subsection{FedProx}

FedProx \citep{li2020fedprox} keeps the FedAvg aggregation rule but
augments each client's local objective with a proximal penalty
\[
  \mathcal{L}_{\textrm{prox}}(w; w_{\textrm{global}})
  \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\textrm{CE}}(w) \;+\; \tfrac{\mu}{2}\,
        \lVert w - w_{\textrm{global}} \rVert^{2},
\]
where $w_{\textrm{global}}$ is the round-start global model and $\mu$ is
the proximal coefficient. The intent is to keep clients' local optima
from drifting too far from the current global parameters when their
data is non-IID. By construction, $\mu = 0$ collapses the penalty term
to zero and the FedProx update becomes bit-identical to FedAvg; this
equivalence is verified numerically by a regression test, so any FedProx
advantage reported later cannot be a coding artefact of a separate code
path.

A working value of $\mu = 0.01$ is used outside the $\mu$-sweep of
§\ref{sec:mu-sensitivity}, which evaluates sensitivity and locates the
empirical peak on this benchmark. Per-client $\mu$ overrides
(\texttt{--mu-per-client}) are used only for the appendix
asymmetric-$\mu$ probe. Crucially, FedProx is \emph{not} treated as a
black-box: the four-condition decomposition in §\ref{sec:li-straggler}
separates the local proximal term from the $\gamma$-inexact
partial-update protocol FedProx is conventionally paired with.

\subsection{FedNova}

FedNova \citep{wang2020fednova} keeps FedAvg-style local solvers but
normalises each client's update by its effective local work $\tau_i$
before aggregation, addressing the objective-inconsistency problem.
It uses the momentum-aware normaliser $a_i = (\tau_i(1-m) - m(1-m^{\tau_i})) /
(1-m)^{2}$ (Wang et al.\ 2020, Algorithm 1 and §3.3), which reduces to
$a_i = \tau_i$ when $m = 0$ as under Regime~B. The server aggregates
$d_i / a_i$ weighted by sample fraction and scales the global step by
$a_{\textrm{eff}} = \sum_i p_i a_i$; this normaliser is checked against a
brute-force reference numerically. FedNova is the work-normalisation
comparator, tested under deterministic LR asymmetry and noisy per-round
$\tau_i$ in §\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry}; both regimes lie outside the
stable-$\tau$ assumption of the original analysis.

\section{System heterogeneity and partial-update protocols}
\label{sec:method-protocols}

\subsection{Straggler simulation}

System heterogeneity is simulated by reducing certain clients' local
work in a given round (computational asymmetry, not wall-clock
latency). The schedule generator has three
modes used in the chapter: \texttt{uniform}; \texttt{fixed\_stragglers}
(specified clients run $E_{\textrm{straggler}} < E_{\max}$ every
round); and \texttt{random\_stragglers} (a fraction of clients per
round runs a random $E \in [1, E_{\max}-1]$). The filename tag is
\texttt{sh-\textit{mode}}. Two 7-client experiments use the random
mode to set up the screening comparison in §\ref{sec:li-straggler}:
\texttt{system\_het\_iid\_random} (IID, $n = 10$, Regime~A) and
\texttt{system\_het\_random} (engineered balanced-paired, $n = 10$,
Regime~A); the FedProx--FedAvg gap reverses sign between these cells.

\subsection{Drop versus $\gamma$-inexact handling}

When a client returns a partial update --- fewer local epochs than
$E_{\max}$ --- the server either \emph{drops} it (excluded from that round's
aggregation) or accepts it under \emph{$\gamma$-inexact} handling
(Figure~\ref{fig:signal-flow-gamma}). \textbf{Drop versus $\gamma$-inexact is
a protocol choice, not an optimiser property:} either policy pairs with either
FedAvg or FedProx, and this independence is what the four-condition
decomposition below exploits.

\subsection{Four-condition decomposition}

The Li-style four-condition decomposition runs on the L4 two-client
partition with Regime~B (batch $10$, momentum $0$), the fixed-straggler
schedule, $E_{\max} = 20$, $E_{\textrm{straggler}} = 5$ for Client~1
(the rare-class holder), and \texttt{--fixed-straggler-ids 1} pinning
the asymmetry deterministically to Client~1; $n = 3$ matched seeds per
cell. Holding the schedule and the partition fixed across all four
cells isolates the drop-vs-accept axis from any per-round variability
that a random schedule would introduce; the only quantities that change
between cells are the optimiser (FedAvg vs FedProx) and the
partial-update protocol (drop vs $\gamma$-inexact). The four cells are
laid out in Table~\ref{tab:four-condition-design}.

The decomposition deliberately omits a FedAvg + $\gamma$-inexact cell.
The result of attributing the rare-class-rescue effect to the
$\gamma$-inexact protocol (rather than to the proximal term alone) is
therefore a claim about the \emph{FedProx + $\gamma$-inexact} protocol
rather than about $\gamma$-inexact acceptance in isolation.

\subsection{Perfect-storm stress}

The perfect-storm experiment switches to a random-straggler schedule at
fraction $0.9$ (so $\approx 90\,\%$ of clients are stragglers per
round) on the same L4 partition with Regime~B, comparing FedAvg+drop,
FedProx ($\mu = 1.0$)+$\gamma$-inex, and FedProx ($\mu = 0.01$)+$\gamma$-inex
across $n = 3$ matched seeds. The straggler schedule/severity and the
protocol arms differ from the four-condition design (batch and momentum
are unchanged), so §\ref{sec:li-straggler} reports it as a \emph{stress
demonstration} of the same ordering at larger magnitude, not as a
causal decomposition.

\subsection{Learning-rate asymmetry}

The LR-asymmetry envelope keeps Client~0 at $\textrm{lr} = 0.01$ and
varies Client~1 across six ratios from $1{:}1$ to $50{:}1$
($\textrm{lr}_1 \in \{0.01, 0.005, 0.002, 0.001, 0.0005, 0.0002\}$).
This weakens the rare-class client's per-round update magnitude
\emph{without dropping it}, separating the ``rare client is silent''
failure mode of the straggler protocols from the ``rare client takes
smaller steps'' failure mode of LR asymmetry. The envelope is the
substrate for the FedNova regime-dependence claim in
§\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry}.

\subsection{Random-$\tau$ FedNova stress}

The companion FedNova stress test (\texttt{system\_het\_random\_fednova})
runs on the 7-client engineered balanced-paired partition under
Regime~A with \texttt{random\_stragglers} fraction $0.5$, $n = 10$
seeds. This is \emph{not} an L4 experiment; the matching FedAvg and
FedProx cells are in \texttt{system\_het\_random} on the same partition
and schedule. The protocol deliberately violates the stable-$\tau$
assumption of \citet{wang2020fednova} by making $\tau_i$ a per-round
random variable. The empirical outcome in §\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry} is
that FedNova collapses --- regime-dependence in both directions.

Partitions and protocols together fix where rare-class signal lives and
whether it reaches aggregation. The next section introduces the loss-side
interventions used to test whether the FedProx-vs-FedAvg gap is a
disguised class-imbalance correction.

\section{Loss-side class-imbalance interventions}
\label{sec:method-losses}

The loss-side comparators test a falsification hypothesis: if FedProx's
heterogeneity-regime advantage is really a disguised class-imbalance
correction, a direct loss-level correction should match or exceed it on
the same partition. Three losses are compared on the L4 substrate:
plain cross-entropy
(the unweighted baseline used elsewhere); weighted CE with
inverse-frequency weights $w_c = (1/n_c) \cdot C / \sum_j (1/n_j)$,
normalised so $\sum_c w_c = C$; rare classes receive proportionally
more loss contribution; and focal loss \citep{lin2017focal} with
$\gamma = 2$,
which down-weights easy examples by $(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}$. The focal
implementation supplies an inverse-frequency $\alpha_c$ vector by
default, so the focal comparator in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is focal
with class-balanced $\alpha$, not plain unweighted focal. Weighted CE
intervenes in per-class loss contribution during local training while
FedProx intervenes in aggregation; if FedProx were really class
re-weighting in disguise, weighted CE would close the gap on its own.
This is a focused comparator set, not a full imbalance study, and is
only run on L4 (§\ref{sec:method-partitions}).

\section{Runtimes and implementation provenance}
\label{sec:method-runtimes}

Two runtimes are used. A \emph{pure-PyTorch reference loop} provides
cross-runtime corroboration for the headline statistical-heterogeneity
experiment. The \emph{Flower simulation} (framework $1.23$
\citep{beutel2020flower}) is the production-grade runtime carrying
every other experiment and all reported headline numbers.\footnote{For
reproducibility, the implementation modules referenced abstractly in
this chapter are: aggregation and the pure-PyTorch loop under
\texttt{fl/} (\texttt{aggregation.py}, \texttt{server\_loop.py},
\texttt{local\_train.py}, \texttt{system\_het.py},
\texttt{class\_imbalance.py}, \texttt{evaluation.py}), the partitioner
under \texttt{data/partition.py}, the Flower strategies under
\texttt{fl\_flower/} (\texttt{strategy\_fednova.py},
\texttt{strategy\_straggler\_dropping.py}), and the guards under
\texttt{tests/} covering the proximal term, the
$\mu = 0 \Leftrightarrow$ FedAvg equivalence, the FedNova normaliser,
straggler dropping, partition determinism, and framework-provenance
fields.} Where the two runtimes disagree on magnitude (engineered
partition, §\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity}), Flower is central and
the pure-PyTorch estimate is direction-only corroboration.

Flower production runs save \texttt{history\_*.csv} (per-round
validation), \texttt{test\_at\_best\_*.json} (selected-round metrics
plus a provenance dictionary --- git commit, hostname, framework
version, Python/PyTorch versions, ISO start/finish timestamps),
\texttt{test\_predictions\_*.npz} (argmax predictions and labels, used
for confusion matrices and rare-to-mel-nevi misrouting in
§\ref{sec:rare-class-collapse}), and optional
\texttt{client\_update\_norms\_*.csv} (diagnostic only). Older
pure-PyTorch headline runs retain \texttt{history\_*.csv} and
\texttt{test\_at\_best\_*.json} but lack full git-commit provenance,
prediction NPZs, and companion update-norm logs, which is why the
chapter treats them as direction-only corroboration. Every numerical
claim in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} traces to raw artefacts or freshly
regenerated summaries, never to stale committed CSVs.

\section{Evaluation metrics and statistical reporting}
\label{sec:method-metrics}

The primary metric throughout the thesis is \emph{test macro-F1 at the
best-validation checkpoint}, computed on the global test set. The choice
is dictated by the imbalance documented in §\ref{sec:method-dataset}:
macro-F1 weights all seven classes equally
and is sensitive to rare-class collapse; raw accuracy saturates at the
$\approx 66.9\,\%$ mel-nevi prevalence floor and would not separate a
model that has lost the rare classes from one that has not. Secondary
diagnostics are: per-class F1; per-class recall, defined as the
confusion-matrix diagonal $P(\hat{y} = c \mid y = c)$;
rare-class F1, the unweighted mean of per-class F1 over $\mathrm{RARE\_IDX}
= \{\text{dermato}, \text{melanoma}, \text{vascular}\}$;
rare-to-mel-nevi misrouting, the unweighted mean over rare classes of
$P(\hat{y} = \text{nv} \mid y = c)$; convergence curves; and per-client
update norms (diagnostic only). Balanced accuracy is stored as a
sanity-check diagnostic but is never used to rank optimisers --- raw
accuracy would mis-rank the L4 5{:}1 LR-asymmetry cell
(§\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry}).

The validation and test sets are global and class-stratified across
every run; client partitions apply only to the training set. Validation
macro-F1 selects the best checkpoint round-by-round; test metrics are
evaluated \emph{once} at the selected checkpoint and are not used for
selection. Last-round and last-$K$ summaries feed only the
protocol-flip audit. Probabilities are not stored
(\texttt{test\_predictions\_*.npz} carries argmax + labels), so AUC and
calibration metrics are not used. The FedProx local objective includes the
proximal penalty in its logged training loss; cross-algorithm comparisons
therefore use test macro-F1, never logged train-loss.

A \emph{protocol flip} is a sign reversal of $\Delta = \mathrm{FedProx}
- \mathrm{FedAvg}$ between the best-validation checkpoint, the
final-round checkpoint, and the last-$K$ mean. The audit is a
robustness check on reporting choice, not a result claim.

Three seed tiers are reported distinctly (Table~\ref{tab:seed-tiers}).
Sample SDs use \texttt{ddof=1}. Within a runtime, matched seeds align
initialisation, the per-class shuffle inside the partitioner, and the
data-loader RNG as far as the implementation permits; cross-runtime
determinism is not asserted, which is why the pure-PyTorch / Flower
comparison in §\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity} is direction-only.
The \emph{node-pinned L4} configuration is a variance-and-provenance
control, not a partition design: it re-runs L4 four-condition cells on
a single compute node with matched seeds to isolate cross-node CUDA
non-determinism from any optimiser effect.

\section{Methods summary}
\label{sec:method-summary}

The chapter has fixed a regime map rather than a test rig for a single
optimiser. The dataset (§\ref{sec:method-dataset}) defines the
rare-class failure risk: a $66.9\,\%$ majority class, two
$\leq 1.4\,\%$ minority classes (dermatofibroma, vascular), and a
clinically high-priority $11.1\,\%$ melanoma class that any model can
ignore without sacrificing accuracy. The client partitions
(§\ref{sec:method-partitions}) control \emph{where} rare-class signal
is located, from IID through engineered and Dirichlet skew to the
class-disjoint L4 substrate and its quantity-only L1 control. The system-heterogeneity protocols
(§\ref{sec:method-protocols}) control \emph{whether} the rare-class
client's updates reach aggregation, separating drop-stragglers
handling from $\gamma$-inexact partial-update acceptance. The
optimisers themselves (§\ref{sec:method-optimisers}) define
\emph{how} local drift and per-client work asymmetry are handled ---
FedAvg's sample-weighted mean, FedProx's local proximal anchor,
FedNova's effective-work normalisation. Loss-side interventions
(§\ref{sec:method-losses}) test whether class-imbalance correction
applied at the loss layer reproduces the same patterns. Evaluation
(§\ref{sec:method-metrics}) is by macro-F1 and rare-class diagnostics
so a rare-class collapse cannot hide behind majority-class accuracy.
The Results chapter therefore does not test a single optimiser
ranking; it follows this regime map to identify when FedAvg, FedProx,
and FedNova preserve or lose rare-class performance.

\section{Statistical heterogeneity alone gives limited optimiser separation}
\label{sec:statistical-heterogeneity}

Under an IID partition (Flower, $n=10$ paired seeds), FedAvg and FedProx
differ by $\Delta = -0.007$ macro-F1 (FA $0.585$, FP $0.578$, FN $0.577$) ---
within seed noise. We treat this as a negative control: statistical
heterogeneity is a necessary condition for any FedAvg--FedProx gap on this
benchmark.

On the engineered balanced-paired seven-client partition we report three
estimates ($n=10$ paired seeds each): Flower paired $\Delta = +0.007$ (7/10
paired wins, full \texttt{git\_commit} and \texttt{client\_update\_norms}
provenance), Flower $\mu$-sweep peak at $\mu = 0.01$ $\Delta = +0.013$ (full
provenance, $\mu$ characterisation in \S\,\ref{sec:mu-sensitivity}), and
pure-PyTorch reference $\Delta = +0.027$ (9/10 wins, no \texttt{git\_commit},
no companion update-norm logs). All three measure the same partition through
slightly different code paths; they agree on sign and bracket the magnitude
at $0.01$--$0.03$. The Flower paired $\Delta = +0.007$ is the central
headline; the pure-PyTorch number is cross-runtime corroboration.

The pure-PyTorch $+0.027$ is concentrated in two minority classes --- melanoma
and actinic keratoses carry $61\%$ and $36\%$ of the macro-F1 advantage --- while
the other five classes, including the invariant mel-nevi, are flat
(Figure~\ref{fig:engineered-per-class}). The gain is the rebalancing of two
minority classes, not a uniform optimiser improvement.

Three further partitions replicate the small-magnitude finding: Dirichlet
$\alpha = 0.1$ and specialist 1-of-7 both give $\Delta = +0.008$ ($n=10$), and
the multi-seed node-pinned L4 control $\Delta = +0.005$ ($n=3$). The single-seed
heterogeneity-ladder pilot at L4 gives $\Delta = -0.030$, but that deficit is one
rare-class (vascular) outcome on one seed and the $n=3$ node-pinned promotion
averages it out. All Dirichlet aggregates use regenerated raw-JSON summaries; the
previously-committed analysis CSVs were stale and showed a spurious catastrophic
FedProx failure that the regenerated data do not support.

On the engineered partition, FedProx reduces test-macro-F1 SD from $0.025$
to $0.014$ ($-45\,\%$), reaches validation macro-F1 $= 0.40$ in $9.8$ vs
$12.7$ rounds ($1.3\times$ speed-up), and shows a paired mid-training peak
$\Delta = +0.094$ at round 50 that closes to $+0.007$ by round 150. These
trajectory characteristics are documented for the engineered partition only.

Statistical heterogeneity alone produces small, runtime-sensitive separation
(Table~\ref{tab:stat-het-headline}, Figure~\ref{fig:stat-het-forest}). The
next question is whether the proximal coefficient $\mu$ itself explains when
FedProx helps.

\section{$\mu$ sensitivity: the proximal term helps only in a narrow range}
\label{sec:mu-sensitivity}

If the small FedProx advantage of \S\,\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity} is
real, the natural next question is whether the proximal coefficient $\mu$ ---
the one knob that distinguishes FedProx from FedAvg --- governs it, and whether
a larger $\mu$ buys a larger advantage under heterogeneity. A five-cell sweep
of $\mu \in \{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0\}$ against the FedAvg
baseline ($\mu = 0$) on the engineered partition ($n=10$ paired seeds per
cell) answers both questions at once, tracing a clear inverted-$U$
(Table~\ref{tab:mu-sweep},
Figure~\ref{fig:mu-inverted-u}): peak near $\mu = 0.01$, dropping below
baseline by $\mu = 0.1$ and catastrophic at $\mu = 1.0$.

The peak is at $\mu = 0.01$ ($0.506$, $\Delta = +0.013$, 6/10 paired wins),
essentially tied with $\mu = 0.001$; by $\mu = 0.1$ FedProx is below the FedAvg
baseline ($0.493$), and at $\mu = 1.0$ it collapses to $0.419$
($\Delta = -0.074$, 0/10 wins --- every seed loses; Table~\ref{tab:mu-sweep}).
The $0.09$ macro-F1 spread between best and worst $\mu$ is an order of magnitude
larger than any partition-level $\Delta$ in
\S\,\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity}.

The $\mu$-sweep peak $\Delta = +0.013$ exceeds the
\S\,\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity} Flower paired $\Delta = +0.007$
because the sweep selects best-of-five on the same seeds; the two estimates
bracket the same small effect at $+0.005$ to $+0.015$.

The practical region of useful $\mu$ on this benchmark is a single decade
around $0.01$. The expectation that larger $\mu$ should be preferred under
stronger statistical heterogeneity is not supported here --- a benchmark
observation, not a refutation of the underlying analysis.

The $\mu = 1.0$ deficit concentrates in the rare and minority classes while the
dominant mel-nevi is essentially invariant (Table~\ref{tab:mu-per-class}) --- the
same rare-class shape as the dropped-straggler and asymmetric-LR failures
(\S\,\ref{sec:rare-class-collapse}), via a different mechanism
(over-regularisation rather than under-aggregation). Because $\mu$ alone is
sensitive and harmful when large, the much larger FedProx gains under straggler
protocols (\S\,\ref{sec:li-straggler}) cannot be attributed to the proximal
coefficient itself.

\section{Under Li-style straggler asymmetry, FedProx's advantage is mediated
mainly by $\gamma$-inexact partial-update handling}
\label{sec:li-straggler}

This section presents the central result of the thesis. Every preceding
section has shown the FedAvg--FedProx gap to be small; here it becomes
large, and a controlled four-condition decomposition attributes that gap
to its true cause --- not the proximal regulariser FedProx is named for,
but the $\gamma$-inexact partial-update handling it is conventionally
paired with. This is what turns the thesis from an optimiser comparison
into a regime map.

Before decomposing the mechanism, we first checked whether stragglers alone
create a FedProx advantage. They do not: in a 7-client IID random-straggler
control FedProx is slightly worse than FedAvg ($\Delta = -0.013$ macro-F1,
$n = 10$), but replacing the IID split with a heterogeneous one under the same
straggler protocol reverses the sign ($\Delta = +0.017$, $n = 10$). The advantage
appears only when slow or partially updated clients also carry non-redundant
class information; the next experiment isolates that mechanism in a two-client L4
decomposition with the rare-class client as the fixed straggler.

We next isolate the mechanism in a two-client L4 four-condition experiment,
with $n=3$ matched seeds (Table~\ref{tab:li-decomp-l4}). In this setting,
Client 1 contains the rare-class signal and is made the fixed straggler. The
four cells separate two effects that are otherwise bundled together in
FedProx-style straggler experiments: the proximal term and the decision to
accept or drop a straggler's partial update.

\noindent\textit{Decomposition:} proximal-only $= 0.368 - 0.364 = +0.004$;
$\gamma$-inexact $= 0.479 - 0.368 = +0.111$; total $= +0.115$. Rare-F1 is
the mean over $\mathrm{RARE\_IDX} = \{\text{dermato}, \text{melanoma},

rare classes: both drop-handling cells flatline rare-F1 to exactly zero,
and only FedProx+$\gamma$-inexact rescues it.
\end{table}

The first two cells measure the cost of straggling: FedAvg falls from $0.485$
(no straggler) to $0.364$ when the partial update is dropped, so in L4 dropping
the straggler removes class information rather than merely slowing training. The
next two identify the cure. Adding the proximal term while still dropping changes
almost nothing (FedProx+drop $0.368$, $+0.004$ above FedAvg+drop), whereas
accepting the partial update recovers nearly the full baseline
(FedProx+$\gamma$-inexact $0.479$, a $+0.111$ gain over FedProx+drop). Of the
total $+0.115$ gain, then, about $96\%$ comes from $\gamma$-inexact acceptance and
only $+0.004$ from the proximal term. Because the design omits a
FedAvg+$\gamma$-inexact cell, this is evidence for the FedProx+$\gamma$-inexact
protocol, not proof that $\gamma$-inexact acceptance is universally beneficial on
its own.

Figure~\ref{fig:val-curves-extreme-gaps}~(A) shows the same result over
training: the FedAvg+drop and FedProx+drop trajectories overlap for all 150
rounds, while FedProx+$\gamma$-inexact separates from both around round 30 and
stabilises near $0.48$.

The perfect-storm experiment stresses the same ordering rather than re-deriving
it: on the same L4 setting with a $90\%$ per-round random-straggler rate (batch
$10$, no momentum), FedAvg+drop flatlines at its round-1 value --- best-val
selects the untrained init, mean test macro-F1 $0.087 \pm 0.049$ --- while
FedProx+$\gamma$-inexact recovers to $0.365 \pm 0.023$ at $\mu = 1.0$ and
$0.491 \pm 0.003$ at $\mu = 0.01$, the latter the largest FedProx--FedAvg gap in
the thesis ($\Delta = +0.404$; Figure~\ref{fig:val-curves-extreme-gaps}B). The
mechanism is the one the L4 decomposition isolated (Figure~\ref{fig:signal-flow-gamma}),
pushed to its limit; the smaller $\mu$ wins because looser anchoring leaves more
rare-class learning signal per round. This is a magnitude demonstration, not a
second causal decomposition: the schedule and arms differ from the four-condition
design, and FedAvg+drop collapses too completely for a clean
$\gamma$-inexact-vs-proximal split.

Finally, the L1 control tests whether partial-update acceptance helps simply
because it is a better generic straggler policy. It does not. L1 uses the
same two-client four-condition design, but the clients have identical label
proportions; the split changes quantity, not class information. In this
setting, all four cells are nearly flat (Table~\ref{tab:li-decomp-l1}):
FedAvg-no-straggler $0.601 \pm 0.010$, FedAvg+drop $0.599 \pm 0.006$,
FedProx+drop $0.593 \pm 0.022$, and FedProx+$\gamma$-inexact
$0.605 \pm 0.009$. Dropping the straggler costs almost nothing, and accepting
the partial update adds only $+0.012$, compared with $+0.111$ at L4.

dropped client carries class information the other client does not have, so
accepting its partial update rescues rare-class learning. At L1, the dropped
client carries no unique class signal, so there is little to rescue. The
straggler mechanism is not generic drift control; it matters when system
heterogeneity suppresses statistically unique rare-class information.

The straggler experiments isolate one axis of system asymmetry.
Section~\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry} asks whether persistent learning-rate
imbalance --- a different axis --- changes the optimiser ranking.

\section{Learning-rate asymmetry reveals FedNova regime-dependence}
\label{sec:lr-asymmetry}

This experiment tests a different form of system heterogeneity from
\S\,\ref{sec:li-straggler}. There, the rare-class client was slow and its
partial update could be dropped. Here, the rare-class client is not dropped:
it still participates in every round, but its learning rate is made smaller.
The question is whether an optimiser can preserve rare-class learning when
the rare client's update becomes progressively weaker.

The experiment is run on L4. Client 0 is the dominant client and keeps
$\textrm{lr}=0.01$ throughout. Client 1 holds the rare classes and is assigned
one of six smaller learning rates, producing asymmetry ratios from 1:1 to
50:1. Each ratio is tested with FedAvg, FedProx, and FedNova using $n=3$
matched seeds per cell, giving $54$ runs in total. Test macro-F1 across the
six-ratio envelope is reported in Table~\ref{tab:lr-envelope}.

The pattern is clear (Table~\ref{tab:lr-envelope}). FedAvg and FedProx both
degrade as soon as Client~1's learning rate is reduced and plateau near $0.367$
macro-F1 by 5:1 --- the rare-class floor, where the model still learns the
dominant-client distribution but rare-class learning has largely collapsed.
FedNova behaves differently, holding $0.486$--$0.526$ across the full 1:1--50:1
envelope; even at 50:1 it reaches $0.498$ against $0.367$ for both FedAvg and
FedProx, a $+0.131$ macro-F1 gap. Rare-class F1 confirms this is not a macro-F1
artefact: it is near zero for FedAvg and FedProx by 10:1 (FA $0.016$, FP $0.010$)
but stays around $0.35$ for FedNova across the envelope
(Figure~\ref{fig:lr-asymmetry-envelope}).

The 5:1 cell is also where the chapter's metric choice earns its keep. Mean
test accuracy across the three matched seeds is $0.753$ for FedAvg and
$0.726$ for FedNova: ranking by raw accuracy would prefer FedAvg, even
though its macro-F1 ($0.375$) and rare-class F1 ($\approx 0.02$) make it
clear that FedAvg has lost the rare-class signal almost entirely while
FedNova has not (macro-F1 $0.504$, rare-F1 $\approx 0.37$). The accuracy
gap is small precisely because both models still get mel-nevi
($66.9\,\%$ of the test set) right; macro-F1 separates them because it
weights all seven classes equally. This is the operational reason \S\,5.1
ranks optimisers by macro-F1 and never by accuracy.

A simple interpretation is that FedAvg and FedProx allow the rare client's
update to become too small. When Client 1's learning rate is reduced, its
local model moves less during training, so the update it sends back to the
server carries weaker rare-class information. FedAvg does not correct this,
and the FedProx proximal term does not restore a rare-client update that has
already become too small. FedNova, by contrast, normalises client updates by
effective local work, which helps preserve Client 1's contribution in this
deterministic asymmetry setting.

The update-norm logs are mechanism-consistent: at 5:1 Client~1's update retains
$0.91$ of its 1:1 size under FedNova but only about half ($0.47$ FedAvg, $0.50$
FedProx), so the rare-client contribution is preserved under normalisation and
attenuated without it (\S\,\ref{sec:mechanism-robustness}).

FedNova's success here is not universal: it fails under random-$\tau$ straggler
heterogeneity, where the amount of local work varies randomly from round to
round. On the same 7-client engineered partition as the
\S\,\ref{sec:li-straggler} screening comparison (Regime~A, per-round random
schedule; \emph{not} an L4 experiment), FedNova obtains only
$0.237 \pm 0.089$ macro-F1, far below the matching FedAvg and FedProx cells
($n = 10$: FA $0.493 \pm 0.028$, FP $0.510 \pm 0.017$). Best-val selection masks
the severity: three of ten seeds already sit at the majority-class-only baseline
($\approx 0.114$, predicting mel-nevi for every image) at best-val, and all ten
reach it by the final round.

This contrast is the main point of the section. FedNova is robust when the
asymmetry is stable and deterministic: the rare client is consistently
weakened, and normalisation can compensate. It is brittle when the amount of
local work is random and noisy: normalisation can then amplify unstable
partial updates instead of correcting them. FedNova is therefore
regime-dependent, not universally superior
(Table~\ref{tab:fednova-regime}).

\section{Rare-class collapse into mel-nevi, and why FedProx is not simply
an imbalance corrector}
\label{sec:rare-class-collapse}


\subsection{Per-class collapse}

The low-macro-F1 cells in \S\S\,\ref{sec:li-straggler}--\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry}
fail the same way: rare-class images are routed to mel-nevi
(Figure~\ref{fig:cross-experiment-per-class}). Mean rare-to-mel-nevi
misrouting (over dermato, melanoma, vascular) ranges from $24\,\%$ in the
best protocol (perfect-storm FP $\mu = 0.01$, $n=3$) to $67\,\%$ in the
worst (perfect-storm FA+drop, $n=3$), and $28\,\%$ even under the IID
Flower negative control ($n=10$) --- a class-imbalance saturation that the
FL protocol preserves or amplifies but does not itself create.

The D1 5:1 cell isolates the optimiser dimension --- melanoma per-class F1 is
$1.20\,\%$ (FedAvg), $6.13\,\%$ (FedProx), and $46.34\,\%$ (FedNova), the same
disease across three detection regimes spanning two orders of magnitude. Because
macro-F1 weights all seven classes equally, losing one class moves it by
$\approx 0.057$ --- the order of the entire engineered $\Delta$ in
\S\,\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity}. The same rare-class shape recurs across
aggregation dropout, learning-rate asymmetry, and the $\mu = 1.0$
over-regularisation cell (\S\,\ref{sec:mu-sensitivity}, Table~\ref{tab:mu-per-class}).

The collapse takes two temporal forms of one mechanism: an \emph{instant}

\section{Mechanism and robustness checks}
\label{sec:mechanism-robustness}

\subsection{Update-norm activity}

Update norms ($\lVert \Delta w \rVert$, the size of the client update returned to
the server) are used here only as a supporting diagnostic, not as causal proof.
Two facts emerge. First, FedProx does what its proximal term intends: on the
engineered partition ($n=10$) the mean per-round update norm falls from $1.523$
(FedAvg) to $1.029$ (FedProx, $\mu=0.01$), a $32\%$ reduction
(Figure~\ref{fig:update-norms}). This is a local-training effect and does not
contradict \S\,\ref{sec:li-straggler}, where the straggler-regime gain came from
accepting the rare client's partial update rather than from the proximal term.

Second, update size tracks rare-class performance only within a controlled
protocol. Pooled across 78 L4 runs, larger dominant-client update norms
correlate with higher rare-class F1 (Spearman $r=+0.634$), but the
Client~1-to-Client~0 update-size ratio does not ($r=-0.045$) because the pool
mixes mechanisms (drop, $\gamma$-inexact, LR, $\mu$, loss). Inside the
asymmetric-LR experiment alone, where only Client~1's learning rate varies,
rare-class F1 closely follows the rare client's relative update strength
($r=+0.80$ FedAvg, $r=+0.87$ FedProx; cf.\ \S\,\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry}). These are
mechanism-consistent correlations, not causal proof; the main claims rest on the
controlled contrasts in \S\,\ref{sec:li-straggler} and \S\,\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry}.

\subsection{Protocol-flip audit}

The protocol-flip audit checks whether the algorithm ranking depends on the
reporting checkpoint (best-validation vs final round vs last-$K$ mean); a flip is
a sign change of the paired difference across these choices. Large effects do not
flip and small ones do: $0/9$ flips in the large-effect settings (Li-style
decomposition, perfect-storm) versus $3/9$ in the small-effect settings. The
centrepiece \S\,\ref{sec:li-straggler} effect ($+0.115$) and the
\S\,\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry} FedNova gap ($+0.131$) therefore survive
checkpoint choice, while the small \S\,\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity}
separations should be read as fragile. Further diagnostics (the
$B$-dissimilarity proxy, training-instability metric, asymmetric-$\mu$ Yao test,
early-warning collapse detector, and small-hospital case study) were also
examined; none is load-bearing for the main conclusions and they are omitted for
brevity. Both checks support the same message: large regime-dependent effects are
robust, small symmetric-protocol differences are not.

\section{Integrated summary of findings}
\label{sec:integrated-summary}

Across the six experiments above, rare-class outcomes track rare-client
signal flow into the global model. FedAvg under stragglers drops the
rare-client update outright; FedProx with the proximal term alone does
not change which clients aggregate, so it cannot recover signal that was
discarded; FedProx + $\gamma$-inexact accepts partial rare-client updates
and recovers the signal; FedNova's $\tau$-normalisation rescales by
effective work and recovers signal under persistent LR asymmetry but
amplifies noise under random-$\tau$; weighted CE re-weights rare classes
during local training but only on clients that are actually aggregated.
The conditional thesis collapses to one rule: in these experiments,
optimiser/protocol cells preserve rare-class F1 when rare-client signal
continues to reach the global model. Table~\ref{tab:final-claims} reports the chapter's findings
with explicit strength labels; five take-aways anchor the regime map.

The five headline findings, with explicit strength labels and caveats, are
collected in Table~\ref{tab:final-claims}: the small, runtime-sensitive
statistical-heterogeneity separation (Flower $\Delta = +0.007$, IID control
$-0.007$); the inverted-$U$ $\mu$ sensitivity (catastrophic $\Delta = -0.074$ at
$\mu = 1.0$); the L4 decomposition in which $\gamma$-inexact handling carries
$+0.111$ of the $+0.115$ straggler-regime gain while the proximal term carries
only $+0.004$, with the L1 control flattening it to $+0.012$; FedNova's
regime-dependence ($+0.131$ at 50:1 deterministic asymmetry versus collapse to
$0.237$ under random-$\tau$); and rare-class collapse into mel-nevi ($24$--$67\%$
misrouting), against which weighted CE on FedAvg ($0.505$) matches FedProx+CE
($0.478$).

Federated optimiser performance in imbalanced medical FL is regime-dependent:
FedAvg remains competitive under IID or symmetric low-risk protocols;
FedProx becomes valuable under Li-style straggler asymmetry mainly because
$\gamma$-inexact partial-update handling preserves the rare-client signal;
FedNova is robust under the tested learning-rate asymmetry but fails under
random-$\tau$ straggler regimes; and across failing settings, the common
clinical failure mode is rare-class collapse into the majority class
(mel-nevi). These results do not establish a universal ranking of FedAvg,
FedProx, and FedNova. They instead map which optimiser/protocol choices
preserve or lose rare-class performance under different heterogeneity
regimes.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0.15em}
\item Single dataset at low resolution (DermaMNIST $28 \times 28$); no
replication on Camelyon, full-resolution ISIC, or non-dermatology MedMNIST
subsets.
\item Mixed federation sizes: 7-client for the engineered headline and
statistical-heterogeneity settings; 2-client for the mechanism probes
(Li 2020 four-condition, perfect-storm, asymmetric LR, weighted-CE,
asymmetric $\mu$, node-pinned L4). No cross-scale generalisation claim is
made between the two sizes.
\item Local-epoch budget fixed at $E = 20$; straggler protocols are
simulated via local-epoch counts rather than wall-clock deadlines.
\item $n = 3$ paired seeds for several strong asymmetry claims
(Li 2020 four-condition, perfect-storm, asymmetric LR, weighted-CE,
asymmetric $\mu$). No significance test is applied at this sample size.
\item The heterogeneity ladder is a single-seed pilot and is reported as
such; multi-seed promotion exists only at L4 ($n = 3$).
\item Runtime divergence between pure-PyTorch ($+0.027$) and Flower
($+0.007$) on the engineered partition limits magnitude claims; only
direction is robust across runtimes.
\item No SCAFFOLD, FedAdam, or FedYogi comparator; the optimiser space is
FedAvg, FedProx, FedNova only.
\item No AUC or calibration metrics; predictions are argmax only.
\item Per-client validation distributions were not logged centrally;
q-FFL-style fairness CDFs and Sheller-style cross-client generalisation
grids cannot be produced from existing artefacts.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

The results are best read not as a contest between three optimisers but as a map
of when each preserves rare-class signal in the global model. Optimiser
performance is regime-dependent for one reason: rare-class performance depends on
whether the rare client's contribution survives aggregation, and different
optimisers protect or forfeit it under different heterogeneity. One rule organises
the six experiments --- rare-class F1 is preserved precisely when rare-client
signal continues to reach the global model. Figure~\ref{fig:regime-map-summary}
summarises the resulting map across the regimes studied.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{F_regime_map_summary.pdf}
\caption{Regime map: a qualitative synthesis of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, not new
results. Each cell records the observed outcome for an optimiser under one
heterogeneity regime --- preserved / competitive / rescue / robust (green),
fragile or no extra gain (amber), collapse or brittle (red), or not tested in this
thesis (grey, used wherever an optimiser was not run in that regime). The map
records only the regimes studied here, on a single $28 \times 28$ medical
benchmark with simulated statistical and system heterogeneity; it is not a general
law of federated learning and implies no universal optimiser ranking. Mechanism
probes are $n = 3$ (directional); Table~\ref{tab:final-claims} gives the per-claim
evidence, strength labels, and caveats.}
\label{fig:regime-map-summary}
\end{figure}

Read through that rule, the findings cohere. Statistical heterogeneity alone
separates FedAvg and FedProx only slightly and runtime-sensitively
(\S\ref{sec:statistical-heterogeneity}), too fragile to justify a universal
preference, and the proximal coefficient is sharply tuned: useful near
$\mu = 0.01$ but harmful at $\mu = 1.0$, where it damages exactly the rare classes
(\S\ref{sec:mu-sensitivity}). This already indicates that the proximal term acts
on local drift, not on whether a client's signal is admitted. The L4/L1
decomposition makes the point precise (\S\ref{sec:li-straggler}): under straggler
asymmetry the FedProx advantage of about $+0.115$ macro-F1 comes almost entirely
($+0.111$) from $\gamma$-inexact acceptance of the straggler's partial update and
only $+0.004$ from the proximal term, while the matched L1 control --- where the
suppressed client carries nothing unique --- collapses the effect, confirming that
admitting the partial update matters only when that client is the sole source of
rare-class signal. FedNova is the same rule from another angle
(\S\ref{sec:lr-asymmetry}): work-normalisation restores a consistently weakened
client under deterministic learning-rate asymmetry (an advantage of about
$+0.131$ macro-F1 at the most extreme ratio) but amplifies noise and collapses to
roughly $0.237$ under random, round-to-round straggling. Finally, class-weighted
cross-entropy matches or exceeds FedProx with plain cross-entropy on the symmetric
partition (\S\ref{sec:rare-class-collapse}), locating imbalance correction as a
loss-side layer distinct from the aggregation-and-protocol layer where FedProx
acts; the two address different failure modes and could in principle be combined.

These findings refine rather than contradict prior work. FedAvg
\citep{mcmahan2017fedavg} behaves as expected when clients are well matched; for
FedProx \citep{li2020fedprox}, the decomposition indicates --- within this
benchmark and this simulated protocol --- that the straggler-regime benefit is
carried mainly by inexact-update acceptance rather than by the proximal term, an
attribution to the FedProx-plus-$\gamma$-inexact protocol as configured here
rather than a claim that the method underperforms; and for FedNova
\citep{wang2020fednova} the random-$\tau$ collapse is consistent with operating
outside its stable-work assumption, not with a flaw in its analysis. No result
here should be read as refuting these analyses. The clinical reading stays
cautious too: rare lesions collapsing into melanocytic nevi is the shared failure
mode, but DermaMNIST is a $28 \times 28$ benchmark, so this is clinically
motivated, not validated.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The mechanism probes use three
matched seeds and are reported as directional; stragglers are simulated through
local-epoch counts rather than wall-clock deadlines; the two runtimes disagree in
magnitude on the engineered partition, so only its direction is robust; the study
uses a single low-resolution dataset and mixes two federation sizes; it omits
variance-reduced and adaptive comparators such as SCAFFOLD
\citep{karimireddy2020scaffold} and adaptive federated optimisers
\citep{reddi2021adaptive}; and evaluation is by argmax predictions only. The
thesis therefore establishes neither a universal ranking nor a clinically
validated claim. The map still gives regime-indexed guidance: under near-IID
conditions FedAvg is competitive; under statistical heterogeneity alone the
FedProx gain is small and the proximal coefficient should be kept small; when
stragglers coincide with a client holding unique rare-class signal, FedProx with
$\gamma$-inexact acceptance is valuable; under deterministic learning-rate
asymmetry FedNova is promising but should not be assumed safe under random
straggling; and under severe imbalance, per-class metrics should be reported and
weighted cross-entropy considered as a competitive complement.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

This thesis asked not whether a federated optimiser is best for imbalanced medical
image classification but \emph{when} each preserves rare-class performance. The
answer is a regime map governed by a single rule: rare-class performance is
preserved when rare-client signal reaches the global model. Statistical
heterogeneity alone yields only a fragile FedProx--FedAvg separation (RQ1); under
straggler asymmetry the FedProx advantage is carried mainly by $\gamma$-inexact
partial-update handling rather than the proximal term, and only when the
suppressed client holds unique rare-class signal (RQ2); FedNova is
regime-dependent, robust under deterministic learning-rate asymmetry but brittle
under random stragglers (RQ3); and rare-class collapse into melanocytic nevi is
the common failure mode, with weighted cross-entropy showing the FedProx advantage
is not class-imbalance correction in disguise (RQ4). Future work should combine
$\gamma$-inexact aggregation with a weighted-cross-entropy local objective, which
were tested only separately here; validate the map on higher-resolution and
additional datasets with real wall-clock straggler protocols; add variance-reduced
and adaptive optimisers; and log per-client validation to quantify fairness across
sites. In imbalanced medical federated learning, the pertinent question is not
which optimiser to use but which optimiser and protocol preserve rare-class signal
under the heterogeneity at hand: optimiser choice is regime-dependent, and it is
the regime, not the method's name, that should drive the decision.
